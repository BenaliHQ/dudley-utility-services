Where the design needs something real to hold onto, reach here first.

Standing client instruction from Brent, and it differs from Dudley Land Company, which does sell renewables land services. Two live traps: the existing services-hero background is a **recolored solar array** (panel rows visible on zoom), and `renewables.png` on the live site does double duty as both a panel image and a faint background watermark.

We're a spawn."* Dudley Land Company earned the 45+ years, and DUS runs on that infrastructure. Where the history is the point, Dudley Land Company gets named.
- **Never use oil and gas vocabulary or imagery.** No upstream, midstream, minerals, leases, derricks, refineries, or smokestacks. That's the sibling company's business and using it reads as not knowing the difference.
- **Never invent a number.** Two claims are approved without a source: **45+ years of energy-land expertise** (Dudley Land Company, founded 1980) and **licensed in roughly 38 states** (keep the hedge, never an exact count). ## The one thing that should govern every design decision

**This brand is a credibility instrument, not a sales instrument.**

Utility land services is an invitation-only market. Work arrives through approved vendor lists and referrals between right-of-way directors who meet twice a year and trade vendor reviews. Regulated buyers can't accept entertainment — Garrett says not even a Christmas card — so there's no dinners-and-golf playbook to support. Nobody is going to find DUS through search and convert.

What actually happens: Garrett has a conversation, and the person he spoke to looks the company up. His words: *"the first thing they're — well, who are you? And then they're going to go look on the website."*

So every asset is answering one unspoken question from a skeptical, structured professional who was referred here: **is this a real company that can run my program?** Garrett names the risk himself: *"the true pitch is who is Dudley? Nobody in utility hardly knows who they are. Every single person that I've talked to so far have never even heard of."*

Two consequences for design.

**It has to look institutional, not emergent.** Not polished-startup, not agency-fresh, not "new brand energy." A company with 45+ years of infrastructure behind it, applying that to utility work. Anything that reads as a launch works against the single most important message.

**There are two audiences on the same surfaces.** The utility buyer comes first: a right-of-way manager, supervisor, or lead who decides who gets on a master service agreement. But landowners look too, and they're checking something different. Garrett: *"they want to know you're legit, because there's so many scams out there."* A landowner who just had someone knock on their door is verifying that person represents a real company. That's a different kind of legibility than impressing a buyer, and it's a real constraint rather than a nice-to-have.

### The artifacts of the work

DUS's actual product is a paper trail, and it's an unusually well-specified one. The RFP and statement-of-work audit found the same deliverable structure across four different utilities' contract documents, so this is the shape of the work rather than one client's preference.

| Artifact | What it actually is | Why it's design-relevant |
|---|---|---|
| **Ownership line list** | One row per parcel, carrying deed reference, tract number, acreage, and current status | The single most concrete expression of "we know where your corridor stands." It's a table, and it's theirs. |
| **Weekly progress report with a progress map** | Named risks, per-parcel movement, sent weekly for the life of a program | The recurring rhythm of the work. Status that moves on dated events, not on someone remembering to update it. |
| **Survey plat** | The surveyed drawing of the corridor on a specific property | Already the conceptual origin of the design system's grid. The real object has more in it than the abstraction does. |
| **Title documents and chain of title** | Ownership research, going back 100 years on parcels headed for condemnation | Depth and age. The 100-year search is a striking fact about how far back this work reaches. |
| **The drawing handed to the landowner** | A drawing showing the line on *that owner's* property, handed over alongside a written offer and a plain description of the rights | Statutory good-faith requirement in many states, treated as the floor everywhere. It's the one design artifact DUS puts in a stranger's hands. |
| **Acquisition check request** | The payment mechanism to the landowner | The transaction actually completing. |
| **The estimate** | Built parcel by parcel, with market study and per-acre valuation behind it, and DUS is contractually bound to it | Brent's stated differentiator is how dialed-in the estimate is. It's a document treated as a deliverable. |
| **Kudu** | Dudley's land management system, carried over from Dudley Land Company by name | The named technology asset. Garrett wants it presented the way his last company presented theirs. Public name not yet settled. |

### The dimensions, and they're specific

Transmission land work is specified to the foot. These are real conventions, not illustrations.

- **100 feet cross-country, narrowing to 70 feet where the line parallels a road right-of-way.** The common convention on a transmission corridor.
- **Blanket easement converted to a defined easement.** A blanket easement covers an entire property with no stated location; a defined easement specifies exact position and dimension. Converting one to the other is core DUS work and it's Garrett's own example: *"there'll be a blanket easement across a property and we want to define that easement from a blanket to 100 foot rideway."* It benefits both sides — the landowner regains certainty about what they can build and where, the utility gets a defensible surveyed corridor.
- **Permanent easement plus temporary construction easement.** Two separate negotiations, often on the same parcel. The permanent corridor stays for the life of the line; the temporary one is extra working room (laydown yards, crane pads, access routes) that reverts to the landowner when construction finishes.
- **Roughly 10 acres** for a substation site, assembled by approaching landowners in a target area.
- **Surface rights only.** Never minerals. In Texas and Oklahoma minerals are frequently severed from surface ownership, and staying surface-only is a real distinction from Dudley Land Company's work.

### The voltage ladder

Garrett's own enumeration, 7/30: **69, 138, 230, 345, 500, 765 kV.** *"That's your big boys, right?"* Transmission is the high-voltage backbone moving bulk power between generation and substations. Distribution is what comes off the back side of a substation and feeds homes and businesses.

That ladder is transmission's own number set, and the physical difference across it is enormous. Design-relevant because it's a real ordered scale the audience reads fluently and nobody outside the industry recognizes.

The structural sequence in his words: transmission pushes power to a substation, the substation converts it to distribution, distribution feeds businesses and homes. Smaller substations come off major substations; there are switchyards. Transmission is *"very very regulated"*; distribution is *"more wild and woolly"* and *"a lot looser."* The two are separate departments inside the utility and they behave differently.

**Handle with care:** DUS serves both transmission and distribution. Jenny already cut an explicit transmission-versus-distribution contrast from the messaging pillar because it read as criticism of distribution work. The ladder and the sequence are usable; the comparison isn't.

### The counting logic — this is the most ownable idea in the whole engagement

**Landowner count drives cost and schedule. Line miles don't.**

Garrett's example, and he brought it up unprompted as the thing he had to explain to his own project management team: two nine-mile lines. Identical on a map. One crossed 10 parcels and cost about $70,000 in land work. The other crossed 250 and cost about $600,000. The per-landowner work is the same either way — title, contact, offer, negotiation, closing, recording — so the parcel count is the whole variable.

A related one: a 12-mile rebuild that carried **250 encroachments** to negotiate out of the existing right-of-way.

Why this matters more than anything else here: the competitor scan found that **nobody in the category names a cost driver.** This is a counting idea, it's counterintuitive, it's true, and the field is empty on it. Anything that makes the per-parcel unit of work visible is territory no competitor currently occupies. (Use the shape, not the dollar figures — those are Garrett's unpublished numbers.)

### The physical and human texture, beyond that one frame

Everything DUS does happens on somebody's private property, one owner at a time. The approved imagery list is real fieldwork only: courthouses, working farms and ranches, title documents, survey equipment, transmission corridors and substations in landscape context. Explicitly excluded: boardrooms, refineries, smokestacks, disaster imagery, obvious stock, other brands' logos. That list is a floor, not a target — clearing it is the minimum, not the goal.

Concrete things that appear in the transcripts as ordinary operational detail:

- **48-hour notice to a landowner** before vehicles cross their property.
- **Gate access and locked gates** — a real logistics item, negotiated and documented.
- **Being the landowner's phone call.** DUS is the number that answers, instead of the excavator operator.
- **The five-point first conversation** with each landowner: the project's purpose, where the study corridor may sit, what survey crews will actually do on the property, the construction timeline, and the access details the crew needs.
- **Open houses** during routing and siting, before the regulatory approval exists.
- **Landowners driving out to the site** to confirm the person who knocked was real.
- **Vegetation management as a notification process**, not the clearing itself — DUS gets a memorandum of understanding that the landowner was told crews are coming.
- **Condemnation prepared in parallel** with voluntary negotiation, rather than after it fails, so no single holdout controls the in-service date.

### The vocabulary, used without explanation

The audience does this work daily. **Defining industry basics reads as talking down to them** and it's on the never list. Terms that get used flat, with no gloss: easement, right-of-way, corridor, parcel, tract, landowner, survey permission, title research, good-faith offer, condemnation, eminent domain, mobilization, closeout, retainage, encroachment, in-service date, investor-owned utility, certificate of public convenience and necessity.

**Get the job titles right.** On the utility side, field staff are **right-of-way agents** or **land representatives**. The people running the work are **land managers** — Garrett is explicit: *"we don't call them project managers, we call them land managers."* **Landman is the oil and gas title** and it belongs to Dudley Land Company. Any careers or apply asset has to use land agent or right-of-way agent, never landman.

On the buyer's side, the hierarchy matters because it's who the material is aimed at: **right-of-way manager** decides who works as a vendor in their areas, **supervisor** issues the work, **lead** runs the work. Garrett: the lead is *"probably the most critical point of contact for us."*

## The generic-elimination test

The voice work uses three tests. Two of them transfer directly to design and are worth running before anything ships.

1. **Logo swap.** Put a competitor's name on the asset. If it still works, it's stock. The category is full of transmission-tower-at-sunset, hard-hat, hexagon-diagram assets that would work for any of six companies. Something in the frame has to be true of DUS specifically — the parcel, the line list, the dimension, the drawing handed across a fence.
2. **Mechanism.** Every claim carries its how. "We reduce risk" is a claim; "we convert blanket easements to defined right-of-way before survey" is a mechanism. Visually, the equivalent is whether an asset shows the work or only asserts competence.

A third, specific to design, from Jenny: **would a utility right-of-way manager recognize this as their own world?** Not "does it look like energy" — does it look like *land rights on transmission corridors*. Solar panels, oil derricks, generic infrastructure, and city skylines all fail that.

And Garrett's own standard, which cuts against over-designing: **clean beats busy.** *"If it's too busy they lose their mind."* He said it about a structured audience he's spent 25 years selling to.
