## What we believe

**The utility is buying certainty, and land is where certainty gets decided.** Engineering and construction run on institutional timelines. Land rights run on human ones: easements get negotiated one owner at a time, and a single holdout can stall the entire line. The right-of-way director is buying confidence that the right-of-way is secured on schedule. *(Garrett's definition, 8/14: the utility supplies the specificity — the approved route, the right-of-way width — and the uncertainty lives in the landowner and the acquisition process. Our certainty is confidence of execution: achieving all necessary land rights, crossing agreements and encroachment agreements included, so the client has legal access to do what it needs to do.)*

**Representing the client to its customers and communities outranks everything, the schedule included.** A utility's number one concern is that a vendor carries its name with integrity to its current and future customers — the landowners along the route are exactly that, and utilities' own websites talk about "customers and communities," not the public. Service runs two ways on a project: the client gets responsive execution, and the client's customers get an agent who shows up prepared, answers plainly, and treats their land with respect. (Garrett and Savannah, 8/14; Savannah read AEP's customers-and-communities language aloud as the model. Ranked above in-budget-and-on-schedule by both.)

**Parcel count drives cost and schedule. Line miles don't.** Two nine-mile lines can look identical on a map. One crossed ten parcels; the other crossed 250 and cost more than eight times as much, because the work happens parcel by parcel and landowner by landowner: title, contact, offer, negotiation, closing, recording. An estimate, staffing plan, or schedule built from mileage is wrong before the first knock on a door. Scope, staffing, and schedule start from the parcel count; pricing often does too, though contracts vary, and lump-sum, time-and-materials, and milestone pricing all happen (Savannah, 8/7). (Garrett's own example; generalize the figures in public copy.) *(Usage, per Savannah 8/12: title and estimating run per parcel, since title has to touch every parcel to find the current owner; survey permission and negotiation run per landowner. When writing about those conversations, count landowners, not parcels.)*

**The right-of-way is secured because landowners say yes.** Almost every easement on a transmission line is signed voluntarily, and condemnation stays the backstop. A landowner who's treated fairly signs sooner, so how our agents treat people is part of how the schedule holds. We tell landowners plainly what will happen on their property, put the offer, the rights, and a drawing of the line in writing, give notice before crews cross, and stay the phone call that answers. The agent at the door knows the area, because landowners in different regions carry different expectations and familiarity builds those relationships best. (Familiarity, never a local guarantee — Savannah, 8/14.) They check on us too: landowners visit the site to confirm the person at their door is legitimate. Copy and field conduct both have to pass that check. In client-facing copy, landowners are the client's **customers and community members** (Garrett, 8/14: "they are customers or potential customers"); when writing TO the landowner, plain language stays, and lead with answering questions and providing clarity about the project rather than "everything in writing" — the writing happens as a matter of course, but leading with it can scare people (Savannah, 8/14). (No DUS-attributable outcome figure exists yet; never borrow or invent one.)

**Transmission is its own discipline.** Transmission work is project-shaped: one line, one docket, a certificate of convenience and necessity (CCN), condemnation authority standing behind the negotiation, and easements bought in acres (Savannah, 8/12: purchases run in acres, not feet; the 100-foot/70-foot figures are width conventions, never purchase units). The work rewards estimate discipline, documentation, full compliance posture, and confidentiality, and DUS builds its transmission programs around exactly those demands. (Never contrast with distribution vendors; DUS serves both lines of business.)

**In budget, not under.** Utility capital is allocated to be deployed, so money parked against a padded estimate has a real cost to the utility, and a blown budget costs more. We treat the estimate as a deliverable in its own right, built with market study and per-acre valuation behind it. *(Weight check, 8/7: how much the estimate matters varies client to client, roughly 50/50. Accurate estimates and few change orders stay true and worth saying; they color the messaging rather than carry it. Certainty, execution, risk reduction, and schedule protection carry it.)*

**In this market, the work is the marketing.** Business development runs on relationship, trust, and performance rather than gifts, and vendor reviews travel director to director. *(Nuanced 8/14, internal-only, never public: dinners, rodeos, and ballgames do happen and clients attend, off the record — Savannah had dinner with a client the night before. Garrett has never spent conference money entertaining and it never cost him work. The point that stands: it's built off the relationship, not off the gift, and performance is what compounds.)* That belief shapes what DUS publishes: material that shows command of the work, written for people who do the work daily.

## What we do

DUS secures land rights for electric transmission projects, mobilization through retainage: routing and siting, survey permission, title, easement negotiation, condemnation support alongside counsel, construction liaison, public relations and community outreach, operational support, closeout. (Routing, siting, public relations, community outreach, and operational support added at Savannah's call, 8/12; this is the internal messaging reference, so the full list belongs here.) Land scope only; a DUS program manager coordinates it while the utility's engineers and contractors do the rest. Surface rights only. In the field that means right-of-way agents and land representatives; running the work, land managers.

The phase walk, each step retiring a specific uncertainty:

- **Mobilization, where the program takes shape.** Ownership list, title work, document preparation, legal review, survey and appraisal requests, and first landowner contact all begin at once rather than in sequence. The estimate and the staffing plan get built from the parcel count.
- **Survey notification and permission.** The first conversation with each landowner covers the project's purpose, where the study area may sit, what the survey crews will do, the construction timeline, and the access details the crew needs, down to locked gates and notice requirements.
- **Title done up front, confirmed to the end.** Title runs to the client's stated requirement at the start — tax card and vesting deed, 30- or 50-year, or full marketable title — gets pushed to survey, and is ready before the offer packets go out. We run it forward through the project to confirm the current owner before easement documents generate and record, and a full interest-holder search backs any parcel headed toward condemnation. (Savannah's correction, 8/14: on right-of-way work title is never "light first, deeper later," and never "continuous" — the requirement is met up front and kept current.)
- **Every necessary right, secured.** The utility arrives with a commission-approved route and a defined line; our job is obtaining every right that stands between that route and legal access — easements, crossing agreements, encroachment agreements — so the client ends with a surveyed, defensible right-of-way. (Reframed 8/14 per Garrett: blanket-to-defined conversion is real work but mostly a developer-side case, not the universal frame; developers say "go from here to here," utilities specify. Blanket easements may return on a future developer-audience page.)
- **Good-faith negotiation, done to the statutory standard.** A written offer, a plain description of the rights being acquired, and a drawing showing the line on that owner's property. State law often names that trio explicitly; treating it as the floor everywhere keeps every acquisition defensible.
- **Prepared for every path.** Voluntary agreement is the goal and the norm. We prepare every parcel's file as though it might take the harder path anyway: full interest-holder search, a final offer documented to hold up in testimony. One holdout stays one parcel instead of moving the in-service date. Condemnation support is a capability we carry, never a place we start, and never a word we lead with (Savannah, 8/7).
- **Construction liaison and encroachment resolution.** Landowners get notice before crews cross, gates get managed, and the landowner always has a point of contact who answers. Encroachments into existing right-of-way get negotiated out; one 12-mile rebuild carried 250 of them. (Never promise one person or one phone number start to finish — Savannah, 8/14: skill sets differ between acquisition and construction, and agents roll to new projects. Continuity of contact is the honest claim; Jenny's phrase, endorsed live: "continuity through construction.")
- **Closeout and retainage.** Files complete, records uploaded, every parcel accounted for. Utilities hold real money behind closeout, and the file discipline that ends a program well is the same discipline that ran it.

While all of that runs, the utility receives the working record of its own right-of-way: an ownership line list carrying deed references, tract numbers, acreage, and status per parcel; weekly reporting with a progress map and named risks; a maintained right-of-way database; field files; title documents; survey plats; acquisition check requests. (Never publish a deadline, threshold, or service level from a client's contract as a DUS standard.)

That record lives in **Kudu**, Dudley's land management system, carried over from Dudley Land Company by name. Utilities require their land vendor to run an operating system that manages project data, and Garrett's standard for it is event-driven: status moves on dated events rather than waiting for a manual update, so the weekly report reflects the file rather than a reconstruction.

## Our differentiation

Every qualified competitor claims experience, capacity, and process. What follows is what survives the logo swap.

**Land managers who stay, and what that buys the client.** DUS cross-trains land managers across the whole phase walk rather than keeping narrow specialists, and invests in compensation in a field where day rates have sat flat for years. The cross-training and the compensation exist to produce continuity the right-of-way lead can feel: land managers who know the whole phase walk and stay, institutional knowledge that stays on the program with them.

**A staffing engine a competitor would have to build from scratch.** Capacity sits on every utility's scoring sheet: can you actually staff this project. DUS answers structurally. Dudley Land Company has recruited and fielded land professionals for 45+ years, and DUS hires its right-of-way agents out of that network while running on the same accounting, HR, systems, and recruiting infrastructure. The network also means the agents on a project know its area, which is where landowner relationships actually get built (familiarity, never a local guarantee — Savannah, 8/14). Scaling for a project draws on an engine that already exists. *(Coverage claims changed 8/14: never tie a state count to licensure or registration on DUS surfaces — license types vary by state (real estate in Utah and Ohio, TREC in Texas, similar in the Carolinas) and a count misleads. The client-facing frame is capability: DUS can work anywhere in the lower 48 and operates within each state's requirements, retaining a broker where a state requires one. The DLC roughly-38-registrations map stays internal context only.)*

**Privately owned, controlling its own pace.** A fact buyers want to know, and the reason the culture investment holds. Private ownership gives the client continuity of ownership across the life of a program, decision-makers who can be reached, and a company free to invest in its people for the long term. Other privately owned firms compete in this territory, so this fact supports the differentiation while culture and service carry it. Always positive framing; never a contrast with anyone's ownership. The fuller ownership and history story belongs on the History page.

**Command of the work, visible in the estimate.** In transmission, differentiation shows up before mobilization. Estimates built from the parcel count, with market studies and per-acre valuation behind them, are built to hold for the life of the project. Holding the estimate is the performance that travels up the client's chain in quarterly reviews, and director to director after that. *(Same weight check as the belief: supporting evidence, never the lead differentiator.)*

## The client's six criteria

Utilities score land vendors on six things. Where this pillar answers each:

- **Capacity and staffing:** the recruiting engine and cross-trained land managers (Differentiation).
- **Safety record:** gap to name. Field standards inherit from Dudley Land Company practice; a documented DUS-specific safety record doesn't exist in our materials yet, and nothing gets invented. Gather what's documentable before the transmission page is drafted.
- **Experience and references:** leadership's 35+ combined years in utility land (client SOQ figure, 8/14: Garrett 25+, Savannah 10+), plus Dudley Land Company's 45+ years, attributed by name. Voltage range is the proof buyers ask for first — "do you have experience with 765?" is the first question out of the gate right now (Savannah, 8/14) — and leadership has executed from 34.5 kV up to 765 kV (Garrett, 8/14, on tape, with instruction to use it). Never imply DUS itself has the referenceable utility record yet; the History page carries that story.
- **Execution plan:** the phase walk. Utilities ask for exactly that artifact twice: in the qualifications package, and again with cost and schedule at final proposal.
- **Local presence:** answered through agents who know the project area (familiarity framing, never a local guarantee — 8/14), inherited Dudley Land Company positioning. Office facts landed 8/14: Katy is the primary location, same office and phone as Dudley Land Company; more offices likely later (southeast, possibly Colorado) pending fall strategic planning.
- **Compliance:** full compliance posture, mandatory legal review, document discipline, complete and correctly formatted submissions, treated as scored behavior because it is one.
