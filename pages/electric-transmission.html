01 · Cost and schedule

What prices a transmission corridor

Landowner count sets the cost and the calendar. Line miles don't. The work happens per owner: title, contact, offer, negotiation, closing, recording. An estimate built from mileage is already wrong before the first knock on a door.

Two nine-mile lines

On a map they look identical. One crossed about a dozen parcels. The other crossed a few hundred, and the land budget ran many times higher. Same mileage, different projects.

What we do about it

We price and staff from the parcel count, every time.

The estimate is a deliverable in its own right. We build it parcel by parcel, with a market study and per-acre valuation behind it, so it holds for the life of the project. Utility capital gets allocated to be deployed, and a padded estimate costs the utility real money. In budget and on schedule is the standard we work to.
02 · Execution

How the corridor clears

Mobilization through retainage, four moves carry the schedule. Each one retires an uncertainty before it has a chance to turn into a delay.

Move one

Title that never goes stale

A light ownership pass tells us whose door to knock on. Title work then deepens through negotiation and keeps running to closing, so every offer goes to the actual current owner. Any parcel headed toward condemnation carries a full interest-holder search behind it.

Move two

Good-faith negotiation to the statutory floor

Every landowner gets a written offer, a plain description of the rights we're acquiring, and a drawing showing the line on their own property. State law often names that trio outright. We treat it as the floor everywhere, which keeps each acquisition defensible.

Move three

Condemnation readiness in parallel

Condemnation preparation runs alongside voluntary negotiation rather than starting once it fails, and we set the final offer knowing it has to hold up in testimony. The point is schedule protection: one holdout stays one parcel instead of becoming your in-service date.

Move four

One phone number through construction

Landowners get notice before crews cross, gates get managed the way the owner asked, and the landowner has one number that answers. Encroachments into existing right-of-way get negotiated out ahead of the crews. A single rebuild can carry hundreds of them.

While that runs, you receive the working record of your own corridor. An ownership line list carrying deed references, tract numbers, acreage, and current status per parcel. Weekly reporting with a progress map and named risks. A maintained right-of-way database, field files, title documents, survey plats, and acquisition check requests.

06 · For landowners

If a Dudley agent knocked on your door

A transmission line needs an easement across the properties along its route. The utility hired us to work that out with each owner, one at a time. Here's what that looks like on your property, and what you can expect from the person you're talking with.

A conversation before anything happens on your land

The first visit covers what the project is, where the line may sit, what the survey crews would do, the construction timeline, and the access the crew needs. That includes the practical details, down to locked gates and how much notice you want before anyone comes out.

Everything in writing

You get a written offer, a plain description of the rights we're asking to acquire, and a drawing showing where the line would sit on your property. Keep all three. Take the time you need with them, and take them to your own attorney or appraiser if you want a second read.

Notice before crews cross

Once construction starts, you get notice before anyone crosses your property, your gates get handled the way you asked, and you have one phone number that answers. If something on the ground doesn't match what you were told, that's the number to call.

The agent at your door is local

We staff agents from the area your property is in. The person having this conversation with you works this region and knows the county, because landowners in different parts of the country carry different expectations and a local agent is the one who understands yours.

Checking whether we're legitimate is fair, and we expect it. Ask the agent for identification and for the name of the utility behind the project. Then confirm it with that utility directly, using the contact information on your bill or on the utility's own website. 07 · Questions

Questions we get

Four questions we get most often, two from the utilities that hire us and two from the landowners we meet.

01 How do you build an estimate for a transmission program?
From a utility

From the parcel count. We identify ownership along the route first, then build the estimate parcel by parcel with a market study and per-acre valuation behind it. Mileage tells you very little about what the land work will cost, because the effort happens per owner rather than per mile.

We treat the estimate as a deliverable in its own right, built to hold for the life of the project. In budget and on schedule is the standard we work to.

02 What happens when a landowner won't sign?
From a utility

Condemnation preparation runs alongside voluntary negotiation rather than starting once negotiation fails, and we set the final offer knowing it has to hold up in testimony. Title work on that parcel goes deep enough to support a full interest-holder search.

Almost every easement on a transmission line still gets signed voluntarily. The parallel track exists so the one that doesn't stays one parcel instead of becoming your in-service date.

03 What will I be shown before I'm asked to sign anything?
From a landowner

Three things: a written offer, a plain description of the rights being acquired, and a drawing showing where the line would sit on your property. State law often requires that trio, and we provide it everywhere we work.

They're yours to keep and yours to think about. Ask your agent anything that isn't clear, and take them to your own attorney or appraiser if you want an outside opinion.

04 How do I confirm the person at my door is who they say they are?
From a landowner

Ask for identification and for the name of the utility behind the project. Then contact that utility directly, using the number on your bill or on its website, and confirm both the project and the company working it.

Our right-of-way agents are staffed from the area your property is in, so the person at your door works your region rather than passing through it.
